The atoms move together in a wave. Creating one quantum of this vibration—a phonon—costs energy, which an X-ray photon can lose.

Motion and energy scales are illustrative. Each mode is a possible scattering channel, not a unique identification from a single peak. Real energies and intensities depend on the material, momentum and experimental conditions.

04 / A crystal writes its own fingerprint

Change the order.
Change the pattern.

Move the atoms, change their spacing, or add density stripes. The diffraction image is calculated from the arrangement you create.

Your crystalReal space
Positions + density9 × 9 sites
Diffraction imageReciprocal space
qₓ: −2 → +2qᵧ: −2 → +2

An evenly spaced lattice produces regularly spaced bright peaks. Increase the real-space spacing: the peaks move closer together.

Elastic scattering from a finite, weighted 2D array: I(q) ∝ |Σⱼ wⱼ exp(iq·rⱼ)|². A logarithmic colour scale exposes weak features. The central beam is masked; q is in units of 2π divided by the original lattice spacing. Random displacements weaken sharp peaks and add diffuse intensity. Stripes modulate density weights every three sites; this is separate from the energy-loss demonstrations above.

Most angles give a weak signal. At just the right angle, waves scattered by successive crystal planes add together: a diffraction peak.

A synthetic first-order Bragg reflection with λ = 1.00 Å and d = 1.20 Å, centred at 2θ = 2 arcsin(λ/2d) ≈ 49.2°. Peak width, background and counts are illustrative. This models an elastic angular scan, not an energy-resolved RIXS experiment.
